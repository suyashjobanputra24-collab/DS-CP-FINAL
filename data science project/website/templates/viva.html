Q1: What is the core data science problem PromiseGuard addresses? ▼
Model Answer:
Food delivery platforms predict delivery arrival times before orders are prepared or couriers arrive at restaurants. A single deterministic point estimate (e.g. "Your food will arrive in 35 minutes") conceals operational uncertainty. Two orders can receive the same 35-minute ETA, yet one arrives in 36 minutes while the other takes 54 minutes due to sudden traffic gridlock or kitchen queues. PromiseGuard's contribution is an integrated framework evaluating: (1) prediction reliability, (2) calibrated 90% uncertainty intervals via Quantile & Conformal prediction, (3) calibrated promise-break risk classification, (4) SHAP explainability, and (5) offline proactive intervention policy simulation.
Q2: Do Zomato and Swiggy not already do ETA prediction? How is this project positioned? ▼
Model Answer:
Yes, major platforms have mature, proprietary ETA systems. Zomato has published blog posts describing kitchen prep estimation, dynamic buffers, and quantile rain models. PromiseGuard does not claim to have invented ETA prediction or replace proprietary platforms. Its value is an academic, transparent, reproducible investigation of prediction reliability, uncertainty calibration, explainability, and decision-support trade-offs on accessible public benchmarks.
Q3: What is the difference between ETA error and Promise error? ▼
Model Answer:
  • ETA Error: Compares the model's predicted duration ($\hat{y}$) against actual observed duration ($y$). Defined as $y - \hat{y}$. A positive value means the model underpredicted transit time.
  • Promise Error: Compares actual arrival against the communicated customer SLA promise boundary ($y - \text{Promise SLA}$). An order can have low ETA error but still breach its SLA promise if the initial commitment was too aggressive.
Q4: Why did tree ensembles outperform regularized linear models? ▼
Model Answer:
Delivery transit time contains non-linear threshold effects and multi-way feature interactions. For example, Jam traffic combined with heavy rain has a compounding multiplier effect on transit latency that linear regression cannot capture without explicit interaction terms. HistGradientBoosting and Random Forest naturally partition the feature space into hierarchical splits, reducing Test MAE from 4.85 min (Ridge) to 3.26 min (56.4% reduction vs baseline).
Q5: What is a prediction interval and how did you verify calibration? ▼
Model Answer:
A prediction interval bounds the plausible outcome range for an individual order (unlike a confidence interval which bounds the average). We evaluated two techniques: (1) Quantile Gradient Boosting ($\alpha=0.05, 0.95$) which yields adaptive heteroscedastic widths, achieving 87.34% coverage, and (2) Split-Conformal Prediction, which uses held-out calibration non-conformity scores to provide a distribution-free marginal coverage guarantee of $\ge 90\%$. On held-out chronological test data ($N=6,838$), our conformal interval achieved 90.33% observed coverage with an average width of $\pm 6.52$ min.
Q6: Can delivery transit time alone predict customer satisfaction? ▼
Model Answer:
No. In our experiments on the real-world FoodHub dataset with genuine customer star ratings, classifiers trained purely on transit and preparation times achieved an ROC-AUC of 0.4615 (chance level). Mean transit duration for 5-star ratings (24.16 min) was virtually identical to 3-star ratings (24.64 min). This empirical negative result demonstrates that customer satisfaction is multi-dimensional—dominated by food temperature, recipe correctness, missing items, and taste—and cannot be modeled purely from delivery transit times.
Q7: Why is a classification threshold of 0.50 not optimal for promise-break risk? ▼
Model Answer:
In operational decision-making, the cost of a False Negative (a missed severe delay that leaves the customer angry) is substantially higher than a False Positive (sending an automated polite update with a revised delivery window). By plotting cost curves on validation data, PromiseGuard selected $T^* = 0.57$, which achieves 90.7% recall (capturing 399 out of 440 delayed orders) while keeping precision at 54.1% to prevent notification fatigue.
Q8: Does SHAP prove why a specific order was late? ▼
Model Answer:
No. SHAP explains mathematical feature attribution within the model, not physical causality. A high SHAP value for Jam traffic indicates that the model adjusted its predicted ETA upward by +6.2 minutes based on historical correlations; it does not prove that road congestion was the root cause, as traffic heavily correlates with kitchen dinner surges and courier assignment latency.
Q9: What does proactive intervention safely mean? ▼
Model Answer:
Safe interventions include: (1) transparent automated customer updates with revised ETA ranges, (2) offering discount or loyalty credits before the promise is missed, and (3) operational review queues for dispatchers. PromiseGuard explicitly rules out unsafe interventions, such as pressuring couriers to speed or penalizing delivery partners for model inaccuracies.
Q10: What are the primary academic limitations of this project? ▼
Model Answer:
1. Public benchmark datasets lack direct customer-facing promise logs and linked real-time customer satisfaction feedback.
2. The simulation is conducted offline; proving that proactive notifications causally reduce customer churn requires live randomized A/B field testing.
3. Courier historical rating (`Delivery_person_Ratings`) reflects general courier performance, not order-level customer satisfaction.
